As a thematic interpretation, Sator in *Tenet* can be read alongside Satan in John Milton’s [[Paradise Lost]]: **both turn personal loss into a reason to destroy other people’s happiness.**

## Satan’s loss

Satan has lost [[heaven|Heaven]] through rebellion. In Book IV, he recognizes his responsibility but refuses the submission that repentance would require. Seeing Adam and Eve happy in Eden intensifies his envy; he chooses to corrupt their happiness. His despair becomes a commitment to evil.

Source: [John Milton, *Paradise Lost*, Book IV, especially lines 32–113, 358–392, and 504–526 — Dartmouth’s Milton Reading Room](https://milton.host.dartmouth.edu/reading_room/pl/book_4/text.shtml).

## Sator’s possessiveness

Sator is dying of inoperable pancreatic cancer and plans to take the world with him. His possessiveness toward Kat becomes a model for his relationship with life itself.

Source: [Christopher Nolan, *Tenet*, screenplay, pp. 67 and 114–115](https://imsdb.com/scripts/Tenet.html).

## The connection

On this reading, **pride, envy, and the desire for control** connect the two characters. They cannot accept happiness or life continuing beyond their own possession of it. Their suffering becomes, in their minds, permission to make others suffer; it does not remove their responsibility for that choice.
