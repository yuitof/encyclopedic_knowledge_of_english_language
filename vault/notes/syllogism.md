syllogism
: A syllogism is a deductive argument consisting of a major premise, a minor premise, and a conclusion.

三段論法 

also see: [[Aristotle]]
## Logical Structure

- **Major premise:** A → B
- **Minor premise:** C → A
- **Conclusion:** C → B

In terms of set inclusion:

$$
C \subseteq A \subseteq B
\implies C \subseteq B
$$

## Role of Each Term

- **A (Middle term):** The core justification of an argument. It is an intermediate category that connects C to B.
- **B (Major term):** The predicate of the conclusion, determined by your assigned stance.
- **C (Minor term):** The subject of the conclusion, determined by the motion.

A is a superset of C and a subset of B. It need not be strictly larger than C, although it generally is when constructing a useful debate argument.

## Constructing Arguments

C and B are determined by the motion and your assigned stance. **The only term you need to come up with is A.**

Argument construction is essentially the process of finding an appropriate A and establishing both relationships:

1. **A → B:** Why does this principle justify your stance?
2. **C → A:** Why does the subject of the motion satisfy this principle?

Different arguments introduce different middle terms while maintaining the same ultimate conclusion.

- Argument 1: C → A₁ → B
- Argument 2: C → A₂ → B
- Argument 3: C → A₃ → B

## Example

**Motion:** This House would ban animal testing for cosmetics.

**Stance:** Proposition

**Argument:** Unnecessary animal suffering.

- A: Practices that cause unnecessary animal suffering.
- B: Practices that should be banned.
- C: Animal testing for cosmetics.

### Major Premise (A → B)

Practices that cause unnecessary animal suffering should be banned.

### Minor Premise (C → A)

Animal testing for cosmetics causes unnecessary animal suffering.

### Conclusion (C → B)

Therefore, animal testing for cosmetics should be banned.

## Rebuttal

There are two fundamental ways to challenge a syllogistic argument:

- **Attack the major premise (A → B):** Challenge the underlying principle or its justification.
- **Attack the minor premise (C → A):** Challenge whether the subject actually satisfies that principle.

### Strategic Note

**You only need to undermine either the major premise or the minor premise to undermine the argument.**

Avoid attacking both premises unnecessarily, as doing so may undermine your own statements or create inconsistencies in your rebuttal. It is often more effective to concede one premise and focus on attacking the other.

For example, if your opponent argues that animal testing should be banned because it causes unnecessary suffering, you could either:

- Concede that animal testing causes unnecessary suffering but argue that prohibition is not justified.
- Concede that practices causing unnecessary suffering should be banned but argue that animal testing does not cause unnecessary suffering.

Attacking both premises is logically permissible as long as your rebuttals remain consistent. You can also use conditional reasoning: "Even if the major premise is true, the minor premise does not hold."

Note that undermining a premise does not necessarily disprove the conclusion itself; it merely undermines the particular argument supporting it.

## Key Takeaway

**Argument construction is the process of finding A and justifying both A → B and C → A.**

The motion and your assigned stance determine what you need to prove (C → B). Your task as a debater is to find an intermediate term A that connects them.

**Rebuttal is the reverse process: breaking either of the two connections.**
